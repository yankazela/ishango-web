## Overview

Selling a property in a foreign country can trigger capital gains tax (CGT) obligations in **both** the country where the property is located and the country where you are a tax resident. Understanding which country has the right to tax the gain — and how to avoid paying twice — is essential before you sell.

---

## Which Country Taxes the Gain?

### The General Rule: Source Country Has Priority

In most cases, **the country where the property is physically located** has the primary right to tax capital gains from its sale. This is a widely applied international principle codified in most tax treaties.

Under the OECD Model Tax Convention (Article 13):

> *"Gains derived by a resident of a Contracting State from the alienation of immovable property situated in the other Contracting State may be taxed in that other State."*

**Translation:** If you live in the UK and sell a property in Spain, Spain can tax the gain.

### Your Country of Residency May Also Tax It

Your home country (country of tax residency) often also has the right to tax the same gain — especially if it taxes worldwide income. This is where **double taxation risk** arises.

---

## How Double Taxation Is Resolved

### Tax Treaties

Most countries have **bilateral tax treaties** that either:

1. **Exempt** the gain in one country (usually the country of residence)
2. **Allow a tax credit** in the country of residence for tax paid in the source country

**Example:**
- You are a French resident and sell a property in Germany
- Germany taxes the gain (source country rule)
- France also claims the gain as worldwide income
- The France–Germany treaty allows France to **credit** the German tax paid → you don't pay double

### Foreign Tax Credit

Even without a specific exemption, most countries allow a **foreign tax credit**: you can deduct the tax paid abroad from your domestic tax bill.

## Country-by-Country Overview

### 🇪🇸 Spain

| Detail | Rate |
|---|---|
| Residents CGT | 19–28% (progressive) |
| Non-residents CGT | 19% flat |
| Primary residence exemption | ✅ Yes (if reinvesting) |
| Annual exemption | ❌ None |

Non-residents selling Spanish property pay a **3% withholding** at the notary as a deposit against the final CGT bill.

---

### 🇫🇷 France

| Detail | Rate |
|---|---|
| Residents CGT | 19% + social charges (17.2%) = 36.2% |
| Non-residents CGT | 19% + social charges |
| Exemption after holding | Reduces over time; full exemption after 22 years (income tax), 30 years (social charges) |
| Primary residence exemption | ✅ Full exemption |

Holding duration significantly reduces the CGT burden. Long-term holders (15+ years) benefit substantially.

---

### 🇩🇪 Germany

| Detail | Rate |
|---|---|
| Residents CGT (Spekulationssteuer) | Marginal income tax rate (up to 45%) |
| Non-residents CGT | Up to 25% withholding + solidarity surcharge |
| Exemption after 10 years | ✅ Full exemption for residents |
| Primary residence exemption | ✅ If used as primary home for 2 of last 5 years |

Germany's 10-year holding rule is one of the most generous in Europe — hold for 10+ years and pay **zero** CGT as a resident.

---

### 🇵🇹 Portugal

| Detail | Rate |
|---|---|
| Residents CGT | 28% (50% of gain taxable) |
| Non-residents CGT | 28% |
| Primary residence exemption | ✅ If proceeds reinvested in new home |
| Legacy NHR | 0% on foreign property (if not Portuguese) |

---

### 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

| Detail | Rate |
|---|---|
| Residents CGT (residential) | 18% (basic) / 24% (higher rate) |
| Annual exemption | £3,000 |
| Primary residence exemption | ✅ (Principal Private Residence relief) |
| Non-residents selling UK property | ✅ Must file NRCGT return within 60 days |

UK non-residents selling UK residential property must report and pay within **60 days** of completion.

---

### 🇺🇸 United States

| Detail | Rate |
|---|---|
| Long-term CGT (held >1 year) | 0%, 15%, or 20% |
| Short-term CGT (held <1 year) | Ordinary income rates (up to 37%) |
| Primary residence exclusion | $250,000 (single) / $500,000 (married) |
| Net Investment Income Tax | +3.8% if income exceeds thresholds |

US citizens must report **all worldwide property sales**, even if they live abroad.

## Calculating Your Capital Gain

The capital gain is generally calculated as:

$$\text{Capital Gain} = \text{Sale Price} - \text{Purchase Price} - \text{Allowable Costs}$$

**Allowable costs** typically include:
- Purchase transaction costs (notary fees, stamp duty, agent fees)
- Improvement costs (renovation, extensions)
- Sale costs (agent commissions, legal fees)
- Currency translation adjustments (if applicable)

---

## Currency Risk and CGT

When selling a foreign property, the gain is often calculated in the **local currency** but you may be taxed on the gain in your home currency — which can differ significantly if exchange rates have moved.

**Example:**
- You buy a French property for €200,000 (= £170,000 at the time)
- You sell for €250,000 (= £230,000 at the time)
- Your gain in euros: €50,000
- Your gain in sterling: £60,000
- The UK may tax the £60,000 gain, not just the €50,000

---

## Tips to Minimise Capital Gains Tax on Foreign Property

1. **Hold for the long term** — many countries reduce or eliminate CGT after 10–30 years
2. **Reinvest in a qualifying property** — some countries exempt gains reinvested in a new primary residence
3. **Track all allowable costs** — every eligible expense reduces your taxable gain
4. **Plan the year of sale** — if you have capital losses elsewhere, offset them in the same year
5. **Consider your residency status** — selling after establishing residency in a low-CGT country can reduce the bill significantly
6. **Claim all treaty benefits** — ensure your tax return in both countries correctly applies the treaty provisions

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

| Mistake | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Not reporting foreign property sale to home country | Back taxes + penalties |
| Missing withholding deadlines (e.g., Spain's 3%) | Additional fines |
| UK non-residents not filing within 60 days | Automatic penalty |
| Not tracking purchase costs | Paying CGT on inflated gain |
| Ignoring currency conversion | Miscalculation of gain |
