## Overview

Moving from the United States to Germany is one of the most complex international tax situations a person can face. The US is one of only two countries in the world (along with Eritrea) that taxes its citizens on **worldwide income**, regardless of where they live.

This means when you move to Germany, you don't simply swap tax systems — you become subject to **both**.

---

## Step 1: Understanding Your US Tax Obligations as an Expat

### The US Taxes Citizens Globally

Even after moving to Germany, you must:

- File a **US federal tax return (Form 1040)** every year
- Report **all worldwide income**, including German salary, freelance income, investments, and rental income
- File **FBAR (FinCEN 114)** if you have foreign bank accounts exceeding $10,000 at any point in the year
- Comply with **FATCA (Form 8938)** if foreign financial assets exceed certain thresholds

### FBAR Filing Thresholds

| Filing Status | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Single (abroad) | $200,000 (year-end) / $300,000 (at any point) |
| Married filing jointly (abroad) | $400,000 / $600,000 |

## Step 2: Establishing German Tax Residency

### When Do You Become a German Tax Resident?

You become a German tax resident when you:

- Register a **primary residence (Hauptwohnsitz)** in Germany (Anmeldung)
- Or stay in Germany for more than **183 days** in a calendar year

Once resident, you are liable to pay German income tax on your **worldwide income** — the same income the US also wants to tax.

### German Tax Brackets (2026)

| Income Range | Rate |
|---|---|
| Up to €11,604 | 0% |
| €11,605 – €17,005 | 14–24% |
| €17,006 – €66,760 | 24–42% |
| €66,761 – €277,825 | 42% |
| Above €277,826 | 45% |

## Step 3: The US–Germany Tax Treaty

The United States and Germany have a **bilateral tax treaty** (Double Taxation Agreement, or DTA) that determines which country has primary taxing rights on each type of income.

### Key Treaty Provisions

| Income Type | Primary Tax Rights |
|---|---|
| Employment income | Germany (where work is performed) |
| Self-employment | Germany (if permanent establishment exists) |
| Dividends | Shared (reduced withholding rates apply) |
| Interest | Reduced withholding |
| Capital gains (real estate) | Where the property is located |
| Pensions | Depends on source (state vs. private) |

### The Treaty Does NOT Eliminate US Tax

The treaty prevents **double taxation** by allowing credits and exemptions, but it does not eliminate your US filing obligation.

## Step 4: Avoiding Double Taxation

### Foreign Tax Credit (Form 1116)

The most common method. You credit German taxes paid against your US tax bill.

**How it works:**
- You earn €80,000 in Germany
- You pay ~€20,000 in German income tax
- You report the income on your US return
- You claim a credit for the €20,000 already paid to Germany
- US tax owed is reduced (often to zero)

### Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (Form 2555)

If you meet the **Physical Presence Test** (330 days abroad) or **Bona Fide Residence Test**, you can exclude up to **$126,500** (2026) of earned income from US taxation.

**Important:** You cannot use both the exclusion and the credit for the same income. Choose carefully based on your situation.

## Step 5: Social Security and Healthcare

### Social Security Totalization Agreement

The US and Germany have a **Totalization Agreement** to prevent dual social security contributions. Generally:

- If employed by a US company and sent to Germany temporarily (< 5 years): pay US Social Security only
- If working for a German employer: pay German social contributions

### German Social Contributions (Employee Share ~2026)

| Contribution | Rate |
|---|---|
| Health Insurance | ~7.3% + supplement |
| Pension Insurance | 9.3% |
| Unemployment Insurance | 1.3% |
| Care Insurance | 1.7–2.0% |
| **Total** | **~20%** |

## Step 6: German-Specific Considerations

### Tax Number (Steuernummer) and Tax ID (Steueridentifikationsnummer)

- Register with the local **Finanzamt** (tax office) to receive your Steuernummer
- Your permanent Steueridentifikationsnummer (11-digit) is issued automatically after Anmeldung

### German Tax Return Filing

- Generally **voluntary** for employees but **mandatory** for freelancers, self-employed, and those with multiple income sources
- Deadline: July 31 of the following year (extended if using a tax advisor)
- Filing with a Steuerberater (tax advisor) is strongly recommended for US expats

## Common Pitfalls for US Expats in Germany

| Pitfall | Risk |
|---|---|
| Not filing a US return | Penalties, loss of passport renewal rights |
| Missing FBAR deadline | Fines up to $10,000+ per violation |
| Choosing wrong exclusion vs. credit method | Overpaying taxes unnecessarily |
| Ignoring German pension contributions | Missed retirement benefits |
| Not reporting German bank accounts | FATCA compliance issues |
