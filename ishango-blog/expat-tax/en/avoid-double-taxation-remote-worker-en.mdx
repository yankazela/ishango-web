## Overview

Working remotely from abroad creates a tax minefield that millions of workers are navigating in 2026. The central problem: **two countries can legitimately claim the right to tax the same income** — your employer's country and the country where you live.

The good news: there are well-established legal mechanisms to ensure you pay tax once, not twice.

---

## What Is Double Taxation?

Double taxation occurs when the same income is taxed by **two separate jurisdictions** in the same period.

**Example:**
- You are a UK citizen working remotely from Spain
- The UK may continue to treat you as a UK tax resident
- Spain treats you as a Spanish resident after 183 days
- Both countries want to tax your salary → **double taxation**

---

## Method 1: Tax Treaties (Double Tax Agreements)

The most powerful tool against double taxation is a **bilateral tax treaty** (also called a Double Taxation Agreement, or DTA).

### How Treaties Work

Tax treaties between two countries:

1. **Define residency** — establish in which country you are "primarily" resident (tie-breaker rules)
2. **Allocate taxing rights** — specify which country gets to tax which type of income
3. **Provide relief** — through exemptions, credits, or reduced rates

### Tie-Breaker Rules (Typical Treaty Hierarchy)

If you appear to be resident in both countries, treaties use this order:

| Priority | Criterion |
|---|---|
| 1 | Where you have a **permanent home** |
| 2 | Where your **centre of vital interests** is (family, work, economic ties) |
| 3 | Where you have a **habitual abode** (183-day test) |
| 4 | **Nationality** |
| 5 | **Mutual agreement** between tax authorities |

### Check Your Treaty

The OECD maintains a database of tax treaties. Key treaties for remote workers:

| Country Pair | Treaty Exists? |
|---|---|
| UK – Spain | ✅ Yes |
| UK – Portugal | ✅ Yes |

## Method 2: Foreign Tax Credit

If a tax treaty doesn't fully eliminate double taxation, the **Foreign Tax Credit** allows you to offset taxes paid abroad against your home country's tax bill.

### How It Works

1. You earn €60,000 working remotely in Germany
2. Germany taxes you €12,000
3. You file a tax return in your home country
4. Your home country calculates a tax of €15,000 on the same income
5. You claim the €12,000 credit → you pay only **€3,000** to your home country

### Limitations

- The credit is usually capped at the **tax you would have paid** in your home country
- Not all income types are eligible
- Some countries only allow credits for **income tax**, not social contributions

---

## Method 3: Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (US Only)

For US citizens and green card holders, the **Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (FEIE)** allows you to exclude up to **$126,500** (2026) of foreign-earned income from US taxation.

### Qualifying Tests

You must pass one of:

| Test | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Physical Presence Test | 330 full days abroad in a 12-month period |
| Bona Fide Residence Test | Full calendar year as bona fide resident of a foreign country |

### Important Caveat

You **cannot** claim both the FEIE and the Foreign Tax Credit on the same income. For high earners in high-tax countries (like Germany or France), the Foreign Tax Credit is often more beneficial.

## Method 4: Proper Residency Planning

The most effective long-term strategy is ensuring you are **unambiguously tax resident in only one country**.

### Breaking Residency in Your Home Country

To be treated as non-resident by your home country, you typically need to:

- [ ] Spend fewer than 183 days there per year
- [ ] Give up your permanent home (sell or let it)
- [ ] Move your main bank accounts and investments
- [ ] De-register with local authorities (where applicable)
- [ ] Cancel gym memberships, club memberships, subscriptions
- [ ] Update driving licence to new country
- [ ] Cut ties to employer in home country (or set up a foreign entity)

### Establishing Residency Abroad

To clearly establish new residency:

- [ ] Register officially (e.g., Anmeldung in Germany, Padron in Spain)
- [ ] Open local bank accounts
- [ ] Obtain local tax ID number
- [ ] Rent or buy a home in your new country
- [ ] File local tax returns from your first qualifying year

## Method 5: Choosing Territorial Tax Countries

Some countries only tax **income earned within their borders**, not foreign-source income. Moving to a territorial tax country removes one side of the double-taxation equation entirely.

| Country | Tax on Foreign Income |
|---|---|
| Panama | 0% |
| Costa Rica | 0% |
| Malaysia | 0% (remitted) |
| Singapore | 0% (not remitted) |
| Georgia | 0% (not remitted) |
| UAE | 0% (no income tax) |
| Paraguay | 0% |

## Special Case: Employer Location vs. Work Location

If your employer is in Country A but you work from Country B, both countries may want to tax your income.

**How to handle it:**
- Inform your employer of your location — they may have withholding obligations in your country
- Check whether your employer needs to register as a foreign employer in your country
- A long-term arrangement may require setting up a local entity or using an Employer of Record (EoR) service

---

## When to Get Professional Help

You should consult a cross-border tax advisor if:

- You have income from multiple countries
- You are employed by a foreign company while living abroad
- Your home country taxes worldwide income (US, Eritrea)
- You are self-employed across multiple jurisdictions
- You have investments, property, or pension plans in more than one country
